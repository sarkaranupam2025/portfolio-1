Executive Summary

Across OECD economies, 32% of jobs are projected to change radically by 2030 and roughly 15% are at structural risk. Inside Siemens — a 300,000-person, 190-country engineering organization — that abstract macro force became a concrete operational problem: HR strategists, L&D teams, business unit leaders, and employees were each asking the same question in different rooms, with different data, on different timelines. Which skills will my people need, and who needs what, first?

I led the design of the digital tool that operationalizes #NextWork — Siemens' five-step methodology for translating business strategy into named, individualized development paths. The distinctive move was treating this not as an HR analytics product but as a service blueprint problem. Eight user archetypes. Four task domains. A handoff chain where one role's output becomes the next role's input. I owned the persona-to-flow mapping, the information architecture anchored on the methodology spine, and the cross-functional alignment with the HR strategists who own the methodology itself.

The published Siemens outcomes are unambiguous: scaled across 120+ workstreams in 30+ countries, with a 703-person Industrial Services unit converted to digital-first skills via a scalable three-month development path, and a Bad Neustadt manufacturing facility where 72% of 550 employees were re-skilled or up-skilled in place instead of restructured. The deeper outcome is cultural: Siemens stopped reacting to workforce transformation and started designing it.

Phase Two

Define —
sharpen the problem until it forces a decision.

Phase 2 was where I made the most consequential framing call of the project. The temptation was to design "an HR dashboard." The decision I led the team to was to design a connected workflow that produces a named output for a named person — because every interview, every shadowed session, and every data point pointed to handoff failure, not analysis failure.

2.4 · Problem Statement

The whole project, in one sentence.

Sabine, HR Business Partner, needs a way to translate BU strategy into concrete, defensible workforce development paths, because today she spends 70% of her time gathering fragmented data instead of making decisions — but currently the tools, vocabulary, and handoffs that connect strategy to action are broken, which results in workforce plans that arrive too late, are trusted too little, and are executed on only 28% of the time.

The Methodology-Shaped IA Argument
Engineering wanted nav by feature ("Dashboards / People / Learning"). I argued — and won — that nav must mirror the mental model already in users' heads, which is the methodology itself. Card sort and tree test results validated the call: tasks anchored to a phase scored 92–95% success; the one outlier (71%) was the most abstract phase ("translate strategy") and triggered a redesign of that entry point.

The most consequential tension on this project was not with engineering or users — it was with the HR strategists who own #NextWork. This section is the honest version.

6.1 · What I'd Do Differently

Three things I got wrong, said plainly.

01
I'd negotiate the methodology-to-product translation earlier, and harder. #NextWork is HR Strategy's signature framework — earned, scaled, internationally proven. They had legitimate ownership. But several parts of the methodology in their original form did not translate well to a digital interface used by non-HR personas. I argued (and partly won, partly lost) that the Trends Analysis phase needed to be radically simplified for IT manager users. We compromised on a "methodology-faithful" view and a "persona-simplified" view living side by side. I still think we should have gone further. The tension between methodology integrity and product usability is the unfinished work of the next version.

Reflection placeholder · final wording awaiting Anupam's specific anecdote and resolution.
02
I'd start the data layer conversation in Phase 1, not Phase 2. Half the project's eventual platform decisions traced back to discoveries we made in Phase 2 about fragmented data, freshness, and shared taxonomy. If I'd run a parallel platform-readiness audit during discovery, I would have saved the team six weeks of mid-project rework and given engineering a meaningful head start on the data integration spine.
03
I'd build the employee view first, not last. We designed top-down (HRBP → Manager → Exec → Employee) because that mirrored the methodology cascade. But employees are the largest population and the most under-served. Building their experience first would have generated faster validation signal and more political support — because nothing in this kind of project moves faster than an employee who can actually see their own future path. Top-down sequencing felt logical; bottom-up would have been smarter.
6.2 · What Transfers

Three principles I now carry into every enterprise project.

A
Methodology-shaped IA is a generalizable move. When a domain has a well-understood mental model — a framework, a process, a familiar methodology — making navigation mirror that model reduces cognitive load more than any other IA technique I have used. The Siemens result validated this conclusively.
B
Persona-aware rendering of shared data scales. One data model, multiple rendering rules. The same skill profile shown to an HRBP, an IT manager, an executive, and an employee is the same underlying object — but rendered in four different visual and linguistic registers. This pattern now sits in my toolkit for any product with multiple stakeholder types and a shared data spine.
C
The cost-of-inaction card. Forcing trade-off visibility into the UI changes the conversation from "what to build" to "what to choose." I use this pattern in every enterprise product where strategic decisions are downstream of operational ones — and especially in any product where "doing nothing" is a defensible default that nobody is questioning.

6.4 · Next Chapter

What's still unsolved.

V1 still relies on periodic skill assessments. The next phase would integrate live signal from work artefacts — code commits, project deliverables, learning completions — to keep the skill graph fresh without asking anyone to fill in a form. This is where the methodology can finally move from quarterly to continuous, which is the original aspiration of Design Principle 6.
