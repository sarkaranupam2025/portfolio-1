PROLOGUE

The two-minute version. For people who only read the first page.

What the project was

Siemens had a methodology — #NextWork — for guiding workforce transformation in a world where 32% of jobs change radically by 2030. The methodology worked on paper. What didn't exist was the operating system around it — the tool that 8 stakeholder archetypes would actually use to plan, decide, act, and measure.

What was broken

Skill data lived in 5+ disconnected systems. HRBPs spent 60% of cycle time reconciling data, 40% making decisions. Executives saw plans 14 weeks after they were needed. Employees didn't even know there was a plan for them.

What we shipped

A multi-persona web product with one data model and four rendering rules. Hero screens: Workforce Map, #NextWork Matrix, Personal Path, Executive Readiness, Cost-of-Inaction modal, Mobile companion. Plus a data freshness system, a persona lens toggle, a methodology stepper, and the named-person card pattern.

What it produced

Bad Neustadt: 72% of 550 people reskilled or upskilled in place instead of restructured. Industrial Services: 703-person digital reskill in 3 months. Scale: 120+ workstreams across 30+ countries. Bianca: cafeteria worker → mechatronics technician over 2.5 years.

What this document is

The messy version. Sketches, sticky-note walls, killed concepts, scribbled mind maps, prototype evolutions, the polished screens taped onto notebook paper. The polished consulting-grade version exists separately. This one shows the thinking.

1.5 · RESEARCH PROGRAMME · WEEKS 1–14

How we built the evidence base. Five methods. 14 weeks.

What we ran, in order

Method 1 — Desk research (wk 1–3). OECD, Bitkom, World Economic Forum reports. 28 sources. Built the macro thesis.

Method 2 — Stakeholder interviews (wk 4–7). 22 sessions, 8 archetypes, 60-min semi-structured. Audio recorded, coded thematically.

Method 3 — Contextual shadowing (wk 6–8). 8 employees followed through a full development cycle. Bianca-style stories surfaced here.

Method 4 — Support ticket mining (wk 7–9). 2,400 tickets from existing HR tooling at Siemens, clustered by intent. Surfaced the data-freshness pain we'd never have heard in interviews.

Method 5 — Quant survey (wk 10–11). n=287 across 4 BUs. Validated which themes generalised. Killed two we thought were universal.

What we deliberately did NOT do

No focus groups. No card sorting at this stage (came later in Develop). No competitor benchmarking before stakeholder interviews — to avoid anchoring on solutions before we understood the problem.

2.3 · THE PROBLEM, STATED ONCE

14 weeks of evidence, one paragraph.

PROBLEM STATEMENT · COMMITTED WK 14

Siemens' #NextWork methodology is sound. Its operating system is not. Eight stakeholder archetypes are forced to share five disconnected data systems through hand-built Excel reconciliations, on a 14-week cycle that delivers plans that arrive too late to act on. Employees are absent from their own development. The methodology cannot scale until the operating system around it does.

→ Design target: compress the cycle, restore data trust, render persona-shaped views, bring employees in.

2.4 · SIX DESIGN PRINCIPLES · OUR CONSTRAINTS

The rules we made for ourselves. Each anchored to evidence above.

01
One data model, four lenses.
Same skill data renders four ways — HRBP / Manager / Exec / Employee — without forking products. Answers: "Same data, three products?"
02
Always show how old the data is.
Trust collapses when freshness is hidden. Every number gets a freshness badge. Answers: data trust 4.2/10.
03
Always show the named person.
"92 reskill candidates" is abstract. "Kaufmann, Richter, Hoffmann, +89" is a meeting. Answers: "I need to know which 3 people."
04
Show the cost of doing nothing.
Every recommendation includes its counterfactual. The status-quo number is always visible. Answers: "transformation framed as cost."
05
Methodology stays visible.
Users always know where they are in the 5-step #NextWork flow. Never hidden behind tabs or menus. Honours the methodology owners' IP.
06
Employees see themselves first.
Their view is a story, not a checklist. Bianca's path = Bianca's story. Answers: "Nobody asked me what I want to learn."

4.5 · MOTION PRINCIPLES · FIVE RULES

Where motion earns its place. And where it doesn't.

ENTRY
Stagger in 80ms
Cards arrive in priority order — exec sees most-important first.
STATE
Cross-fade 160ms
Persona toggle never blinks. Same data, smooth re-render.
FEEDBACK
Instant. Always.
Click → response in <100ms. Even optimistic.
DISCLOSURE
Expand 240ms
Drill from "92 people" to named list — slide reveal, no modal.
PERSONA
Layout shift 320ms
Switching from HRBP to Exec re-prioritises the whole layout.
Rule of thumb: if motion doesn't tell the user what changed, kill it. Every transition above earns the user some clarity.

CHAPTER SIX · REFLECT

What I'd do differently. What this taught me about leading design.

Three honest reflections. Not the tidy "lessons learned" — the actual frictions, the things I got wrong the first time, and what I'd carry into the next role.

01
THE TENSION I DIDN'T SEE COMING

The methodology was someone else's IP.

#NextWork wasn't built by the design team. It was a framework owned by HR strategists at Siemens — five phases, a workforce-planning philosophy, the works council partnership model. When the product brief landed on my team, the assumption was: "translate this into UI."

What we got wrong, in the first three months: we treated the framework as raw material. We re-named phases. We collapsed steps that "felt redundant." We added a 6th phase we thought was missing. And every time we did, the methodology owners pushed back — and they were right to.

The unlock came in week 11, when I sat down with the framework's principal author and asked one question: "What part of #NextWork are you most worried we'll break?" Her answer reframed the project: she wasn't worried about the UI. She was worried that scaling the framework via software would de-skill the HRBPs who had been trained to apply it with judgement.

From that conversation came Principle 05 — methodology stays visible. The 5-phase stepper isn't a navigation widget; it's a contract. Every screen is labelled with which phase it lives in. The product doesn't replace the framework; it carries it.

The lesson I carry forward: when you're designing on top of someone else's IP, your first job is to make their work more powerful, not more efficient. Efficiency, in those situations, reads as erasure. I almost didn't learn this in time.

02
THE CONVERSATION I PUT OFF

The data layer should have been the first conversation, not the third.

I spent the first six weeks of the project on research, personas, journey maps, and IA. Standard practice. What I didn't do — and should have — was sit with the data engineering team in week one.

When I finally did, in week 12, I learned three things that should have been week-one knowledge: (1) the skill inventory data lived in seven systems, refresh cadences ranged from real-time to nine months stale; (2) there was no single source of truth for "what skills does this person have right now" — every team had a different answer; (3) the works council had veto rights over which fields could be displayed to whom, and that list was actively being negotiated.

Every single one of these constraints became a design principle. Principle 02 (always show freshness) and the entire Cost-of-Inaction modal exist because of conversations I had two months too late. If I'd known about the nine-month-stale problem in week one, the entire research phase would have been shaped differently — we would have asked HRBPs about their data trust before we asked about their planning workflow.

The rule I now follow: before you design what people will see, find out what's actually available, how fresh it is, and who controls it. That's not a feasibility conversation — that's a discovery conversation. The data layer is part of the user's reality.

03
THE PRIORITISATION I'D INVERT

I'd ship the employee view first next time.

The instinct on an enterprise B2B product is to start with the power user — the HRBP, the Manager. That's who has the budget. That's who'll champion the rollout. We started with HRBP, and Bianca's view (the employee path) came in V2.

I'd flip that order. The employee view is the thing that made the rest of the product believable. The first time we showed Bianca's path mockup to the steering committee, the room went quiet. Until that moment, the conversation about workforce transformation had been abstract — "703 people in the Industrial Services cohort." When we showed one path, named, with a mentor and a six-stage track, the conversation became real.

The HRBPs ended up trusting the dashboards more because they could see what their plans meant for a person. Execs approved the rollout faster because they could imagine their cousin in that screen. The works council reviewed the product more sympathetically because employees had a view of their own data, with dignity. Principle 06 — employees see themselves first — wasn't just an inclusion statement. It was a strategic unlock for trust across every other user group.

If I were leading a similar enterprise transformation product today, I'd build the affected-employee view in V1, even if it ships last. It changes how everyone else negotiates over the rest of the product.

ZOOMING OUT

The shape of this project changed how I think about enterprise design leadership.

Three things I now believe more strongly than I did before #NextWork:

(a) The hardest part of enterprise design isn't the interaction model — it's aligning the people who own different parts of the truth (methodology, data, works council, business) before anyone ships pixels.

(b) The "named person" is not a feature. It's an ethical posture the whole product can be tested against. Every aggregate must drill to a name, or the product is making promises it can't keep.

(c) Design leadership in enterprise is, mostly, the work of holding a tension visible — between the strategy that arrives from the top and the human consequence that lands at the bottom. The product is a way of making that tension legible, and therefore negotiable.
